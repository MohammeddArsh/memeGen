\begin{abstract}
We study image-to-caption generation for meme templates on \textit{memes900k}
(300 templates, 900{,}000 captions), following DeepHumor. We run a controlled
$2\times2$ ablation over two frozen visual encoders (ResNet-50 and CLIP
ViT-B/32) and two decoders (LSTM and Transformer), reporting test-set BLEU and
perplexity under a shared greedy decoder. At one epoch the decoder dominates:
swapping the LSTM for a Transformer adds $+5.65$ BLEU with CLIP and $+12.37$
with ResNet-50, whereas the encoder choice only matters when the decoder is
weak. After three epochs the Transformer runs cluster tightly (14.38--14.66
BLEU) and both encoder and embedding size wash out. Perplexity roughly halves
with additional training while BLEU stays flat, exposing the exposure-bias gap
between teacher-forced fitting and free-running generation. Our contribution is
a clean, reproducible measurement methodology rather than a new state of the
art.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

Image captioning---describing an image in natural language---is a core
vision-language task. Meme templates are a constrained but semantically rich
instance: a single image admits many valid captions, and the ``humour'' rarely
follows the surface form of a reference caption. \citet{deephumor2020}
introduced \textit{DeepHumor} for \textit{memes900k} (300 templates, 900{,}000
captions), but its public code reports only teacher-forced perplexity, never
evaluates the test split, and its ``beam search'' is in fact unseeded
multinomial sampling. This makes quantitative comparison difficult.

\paragraph{Goal.} We provide a rigorous, controlled comparison that isolates the
\emph{visual encoder} (ResNet-50 \citep{he2016deep} vs.\ CLIP
\citep{radford2021learning}) and the \emph{decoder} (LSTM
\citep{hochreiter1997long} vs.\ Transformer \citep{vaswani2017attention}), using
the \emph{same} decoding algorithm and metrics, and evaluating generated
captions on the official test split.

\paragraph{Contributions.}
\begin{itemize}
\item A $2\times2$ encoder$\times$decoder ablation with frozen encoders and a
single shared greedy decoder (\cref{sec:method}).
\item Test-set BLEU \citep{papineni2002bleu,post2018call} alongside
perplexity, at two budgets: 1 epoch (6{,}250 steps) and 3 epochs (18{,}750
steps).
\item A rendered caption gallery and a one-command inference demo for
qualitative inspection.
\item The finding that the decoder architecture dominates, while behind a
Transformer decoder the encoder and embedding size wash out; perplexity and
BLEU decouple.
\end{itemize}

\section{Background and Related Work}
\label{sec:related}

Early neural captioners pair CNN features with RNN decoders (\textit{Show and
Tell}, \citealp{vinyals2015show}); attention over spatial features improved
grounding (\textit{Show, Attend and Tell}, \citealp{xu2015show}). The
Transformer \citep{vaswani2017attention} replaced recurrence with self- and
cross-attention and became a standard decoder. Large-scale vision-language
pretraining such as CLIP \citep{radford2021learning} provides semantically rich
representations that transfer well to captioning.

\citet{deephumor2020} trained frozen ResNet-50 $+$ LSTM (and a Transformer
variant) on \textit{memes900k}, using teacher-forced perplexity as the primary
metric and an unseeded sampling decoder. We adopt the dataset and the
frozen-feature paradigm but require test-set BLEU with a shared greedy decoder
and explicit ablations. BLEU \citep{papineni2002bleu} measures $n$-gram overlap
with references; \citet{post2018call} recommend clear reporting (we use
\texttt{sacrebleu}). Perplexity under teacher forcing is informative within a
tokenizer family but not comparable across vocabularies.

\section{Method}
\label{sec:method}

\subsection{Corpus}
\textit{memes900k} provides 300 meme-template images and 900{,}000
tab-separated caption rows. We use the official caption-level split
(\cref{tab:split}): 750{,}000 train, 75{,}000 validation, 75{,}000 test. Every
template appears in all splits; scores therefore reflect \emph{new captions for
seen templates}, not unseen-template generalization.

\textbf{Encoders (frozen).} ResNet-50 \citep{he2016deep} yields a $49\times2048$
spatial sequence; CLIP ViT-B/32 \citep{radford2021learning} yields a
$50\times768$ patch-token sequence. Features are precomputed once per template,
so training never decodes JPEGs.

\textbf{Decoders.} The LSTM \citep{hochreiter1997long} (hidden 512, 2 layers,
embedding 256) receives mean-pooled image features as its initial state. The
Transformer \citep{vaswani2017attention} (6 layers, 8 heads, hidden 512, FFN
2048, embedding 512) attends to the visual tokens as encoder memory and ties
its input and output embeddings.

The $2\times2$ design crosses each encoder with each decoder
(\cref{fig:arch}); runs are labelled A--D, with E the CLIP$+$Transformer
embedding-768 variant.

\subsection{Training and Budgets}
All models share the optimizer and schedule: AdamW, learning rate $3\times
10^{-4}$, warmup 500 steps, weight decay $10^{-4}$, gradient accumulation 4,
batch size 32, seed 42, and a 200{,}000-row training subsample. One epoch is
therefore $200{,}000/32 = 6{,}250$ steps. \textbf{Tier~1} uses 1 epoch
(6{,}250 steps); \textbf{Tier~2} uses 3 epochs (18{,}750 steps), matching the
long DeepHumor run. Hyperparameters were screened at 6{,}000 steps by
validation loss (one knob changed at a time), and the winning configuration was
confirmed at full budget; \cref{sec:results} reports the screen--confirm
protocol. \cref{fig:curves} shows the validation and training loss curves.

\subsection{Metrics}
\textbf{Perplexity:} $\mathrm{ppl}=\exp(\overline{\mathrm{NLL}})$ under teacher
forcing over all 75{,}000 test rows; comparable only within this project's
GPT-2 BPE vocabulary. \textbf{BLEU:} \texttt{sacrebleu} corpus BLEU on a fixed,
seeded 5{,}000-row test sample with greedy decoding
\citep{papineni2002bleu,post2018call}---the cross-tokenizer comparative number.

\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}

\paragraph{Decoder choice is the critical decision.} Across both budgets the
Transformer decoder dominates. With an LSTM, CLIP helps substantially at the
short budget, but convergence closes much of the gap at three epochs; with a
Transformer, neither the encoder nor the embedding size materially changes BLEU.
This is the central, checkable claim of the study.

\paragraph{Validation loss is a poor ranking signal.} The screen--confirm
protocol chose embed-768 by validation loss, yet at full budget the BLEU
differences are negligible (14.36--14.66). Perplexity reflects teacher-forced
fit, not generation quality; a BLEU (or human) signal is needed to choose
models.

\paragraph{Encoder and decoder interact.} At Tier~1 the decoder swap is large
and the encoder swap is large only under the weak LSTM; at Tier~2 the encoder
swap turns slightly negative and the decoder gap narrows, confirming that with
enough training the encoder becomes nearly irrelevant behind a competent
decoder.

\section{Conclusion}
On \textit{memes900k}, the decoder architecture dominates: Transformer decoders
reach $\sim$14.5 BLEU versus 12--13 for converged LSTMs, and behind a
Transformer decoder the encoder and embedding size wash out. Perplexity and
BLEU decouple---halving perplexity leaves BLEU flat---the clearest diagnostic of
exposure bias. The main contribution is methodological: test-set BLEU under a
shared greedy decoder, a clean $2\times2$ isolation, and a two-tier budget that
make the claims reproducible.

\paragraph{Future work.} Scheduled sampling or REINFORCE to reduce exposure
bias; an unseen-template split for true generalization; humor-aware metrics
(CIDEr/ROUGE) while keeping BLEU for comparability; larger vision encoders or
end-to-end fine-tuning; and systematic data scaling on the full 750{,}000-row
training set.

\paragraph{Qualitative examples.} \cref{fig:qual} renders gold (G) versus
generated (P) captions for four runs. Generated captions are fluent but often
diverge from the reference, which is expected given the many valid captions per
template and directly explains the low absolute BLEU.

\section*{Limitations}
\label{sec:limitations}
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Seen templates only:} the caption-level split shares templates
across splits, so scores do not measure unseen-template generalization.
\item \textbf{BLEU is harsh for memes:} many valid captions exist per template
and jokes need not share surface forms; BLEU is meaningful mainly \emph{between}
runs.
\item \textbf{Perplexity is not comparable to DeepHumor:} we use GPT-2 BPE
(50k), whereas DeepHumor builds a dataset vocabulary.
\item \textbf{Exposure bias remains:} teacher forcing and free-running
generation decouple; we quantify but do not fix this.
\item \textbf{Frozen encoders and a small budget:} we do not fine-tune the
vision backbones and train on a 200{,}000-row subsample.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Ethical Statement}
The \textit{memes900k} dataset is used for non-commercial academic research
under its intended use; no human annotators were involved and no new personal
data were collected. All models are trained from scratch on frozen, publicly
available pretrained features, so the compute footprint is modest (Apple
Silicon MPS, tens of GPU-hours). We reuse the open-source DeepHumor codebase in
line with its license and cite it accordingly. Meme generation can reproduce or
amplify offensive stereotypes present in the source data; we therefore report
aggregate metrics only and do not deploy the model.
